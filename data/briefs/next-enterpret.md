## What they are testing

- Founder fit: Do you think like them? Can you anticipate what they need before they ask?

- Honest communication: Will you tell them hard truths, or just say what they want to hear?

## Lines to land

1. You own systems and processes, not just tasks. At Kinage, you didn't just deploy; you built repeatable playbooks that let teams scale without you. That's Chief of Staff thinking.

2. You're comfortable with incomplete specs and ambiguous scope. You ship, measure adoption, and adjust. You don't wait for perfect information.

3. You've worked in regulated or high-stakes environments where failure is visible and costly. You know what a production quality bar looks like and how to protect it.

4. You lead by coaching and ownership, not by title. You've made engineers better at their jobs without being their manager.

5. You speak risk and outcomes clearly, not emotion. When things are hard, you say so and propose alternatives, not excuses.

6. You're honest about what you don't know. You have depth in product and forward-deployed AI. You don't fake domain expertise you don't have.

## Stories to have ready

### Kinage: From zero to twelve institutions

Situation: Brought in to own multi-account AI deployment. No playbooks, inconsistent delivery, cost-to-serve was unsustainable.

Decision: Built repeatable deployment playbooks. Coached field engineers to own quality gates and precision. Made cost-to-serve visible and measurable.

Result: Scaled to twelve institutions. Cost-to-serve down about 62 percent. Team became self-sufficient. This was team leverage, not solo heroics.

### Bank / regulated trust: Eval gates and production quality

Situation: Worked in regulated environment where field failures were visible and costly. Precision and eval gates were non-negotiable.

Decision: Built eval frameworks into product. Fielded failure modes back to Product. Made the quality bar explicit.

Result: Reduced downstream risk. Teams understood why precision mattered. Production quality became a system, not a hope.

### Incomplete-info shipping: Cut scope, ship, measure

Situation: Specs were incomplete. Scope was unclear. Pressure to move.

Decision: Cut scope ruthlessly. Shipped what was measurable. Measured adoption and user behavior.

Result: Learned faster. Adjusted based on real signal, not guesses. Avoided building things nobody needed.

### Judgment under pressure: TD-style pushback

Situation: Faced pushback on delivery timeline. Pressure to commit to dates that felt risky.

Decision: Stayed calm. Proposed alternative: ship core first, measure, then expand. Spoke risk and outcomes clearly, not emotion.

Result: Delivered on time. Protected quality. Built trust that you'd tell the truth, not just say yes.

## Don't

- Overclaim domain expertise in areas you don't have (gov, MENA, specific verticals). Say "I'd learn that" if asked.
- Talk about yourself as a solo hero. Emphasize team leverage and systems.
- Ramble or fill silence. Pause. Listen.
- Agree to everything. If something sounds risky or unclear, say so.
- Use jargon without explaining it. Assume they know the context better than you do.

## Loop map

First round: Understand the role and what's broken. Listen more than you pitch.

Second round (if invited): Dig into specific problems. Show how you'd approach them. Ask about team dynamics and founder working style.

Final round (if invited): Meet the full team. Demonstrate you can work with them. Clarify expectations and decision-making authority.
