They need a PM who ships under incomplete specs, coaches technical teams in the field, and translates customer friction into product signals. You have done this at Kinage. Make it concrete and calm.

- Regulated / production quality mindset. Invisible's customers are often in finance, legal, compliance. Failure modes matter. You have bank eval-gate thinking.
- Customer empathy and field sensing. You deployed AI to 12 institutions. You know what breaks in production and how to feed it back to Product.

## Lines to land

1. You own the full loop from customer signal to product decision. At Kinage, you didn't wait for perfect specs. You shipped features, measured adoption, and fed failure modes back to the roadmap. That's the rhythm Invisible needs.

2. You have built playbooks for deploying AI at scale. Twelve institutions, different workflows, different trust levels. You know what breaks and how to coach teams through it. You can do that for Invisible's customer base.

3. You think like a regulated business. You've worked in environments where precision, audit trails, and failure modes are not edge cases. They're the product. You know how to translate that into requirements without slowing down shipping.

4. You lead by ownership and coaching, not headcount. You grew delivery capacity at Kinage by unblocking engineers and building shared understanding. You don't need a big org chart to move fast.

5. You're honest about what you don't know. You have not worked in government or MENA markets. But you learn fast and you ask the right questions. You know how to operate in new domains.

6. You ship incomplete. You cut scope, measure what matters, and iterate. That's how you got cost-to-serve down 62 percent at Kinage. You know the difference between shipping and perfecting.

## Stories to have ready

### Kinage: Shipping under incomplete specs

Situation: You inherited a multi-account AI deployment roadmap with vague customer requirements. Different institutions had different workflows. You had incomplete data on what would actually work in production.

Decision: You cut scope to the smallest shippable unit. You picked one workflow, shipped a basic version, and measured adoption and failure modes in the field. You did not wait for perfect specs.

Result: You learned what actually broke. You fed those signals back to Product. You built a playbook for rolling out features across accounts. Cost-to-serve went down 62 percent because you optimized for what customers actually used, not what you guessed they wanted.

### Bank / regulated environment: Precision and failure modes

Situation: You worked in a regulated trust environment where eval gates and production quality bars were non-negotiable. A feature shipped with a precision issue. It didn't fail catastrophically, but it was wrong in a way that mattered to compliance.

Decision: You did not hide it. You surfaced the failure mode, documented it, and worked with the team to understand the root cause. You fed it back to Product as a hard requirement for the next iteration.

Result: The team built a better eval gate. You learned how to translate regulatory friction into product signals. You now know how to ship fast without cutting corners on precision.

### Judgment under pressure: TD-style pushback

Situation: A customer or stakeholder pushed hard for a feature that you believed would break the system or delay a more important ship. You had incomplete data and time pressure.

Decision: You stayed calm. You did not argue emotion. You laid out the trade-off: ship this now, delay that. You named the risk and the outcome. You offered an alternative that protected delivery.

Result: The stakeholder understood the choice. You shipped on time. You built trust by being honest about constraints, not by saying yes to everything.

## Questions to ask

1. How do you think about the balance between shipping fast and maintaining precision in customer workflows? What does your current deployment cadence look like?

2. Tell me about a customer failure mode that changed how you think about the product. How did you feed that back into the roadmap?

3. What does the PM role look like day-to-day here? How much time do you spend with customers versus the engineering team versus leadership?

## Don't

- Overclaim domain depth in government or MENA markets. You don't have it. Say so if asked.
- Use fake metrics or invented companies.
- Ramble about AI philosophy or market trends. Stay concrete.
- Claim formal manager headcount you don't have. Talk about coaching and leverage instead.
- Hide gaps. Name them and show how you learn.
- Pretend you ship perfect specs. You don't. You ship incomplete and measure.
